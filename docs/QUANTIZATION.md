# Quantising real-valued ML data into `F_q` for ICC

Week 3 design document (Fall 2026 plan). Covers the survey of candidate embeddings, the
choice of field size, the proof obligation behind "decodable", and the entropy side of the
same knob. Every number is regenerated by

```bash
.venv/bin/python tools/quantization_study.py --csv results/quantization_budget.csv
```

Implementation: `icc/quantize.py`. Verified end to end against the real scheme (study D).

---

## 1. Why this is not the standard fixed-point-in-MPC problem

Embedding reals in a finite field is well-trodden in secure computation. Catrina–Saxena
(FC 2010) fixed the standard recipe — scale by `2^f`, round, work with balanced residues —
and CrypTen, SPDZ and the SecAgg line all use variants of it. In all of that work the field
size is constrained by **one** thing: dynamic range. Pick `q` large enough that nothing
wraps, and you are done; larger `q` is only a performance cost.

ICC breaks that. Here `q` appears in the privacy analysis too, through Theorem 1:

```
m ≥ n + p + log_q(1/ε) − H_p(X) + max_R H_p(X_R),   λ = dim RM_q(d,m) workers
```

The entropy terms are in `log_q` units, so the per-symbol entropy is a **ratio**

```
h = H_p(symbol in bits) / log2 q
```

Two separate steps are bundled in that line, and they are worth keeping apart because only
one of them is free:

- **The division by `log2 q` is a change of base, not a modelling choice.** Appendix A of
  the ICC paper defines `H_p(X) = 1/(p−1) · log_q( Σ_x p^p(x) )` — the logarithm is base
  `q`, which is also what Theorem 1's `log_q(1/ε)` and the `H_p(X) ≤ n` scale of Example 1
  (`H_p(X) = n−1`, `max_R H_p(X_R) = r−1`) require for dimensional consistency. Since
  `log_q z = log2 z / log2 q`, any entropy expressed in bits divides by `log2 q` to enter
  the bound. `utils.compute_symbol_p_entropy` already computes in base `q`
  (`math.log(sum_pp, q)`); `quantize.entropy_bounds` does the conversion explicitly at
  `h_lower_bits / log2 q`, because the bounds of §6 are naturally derived in bits.
- **Calling `h` "per-symbol" and writing `H_p(X) = n·h` is the i.i.d. assumption**, since it
  needs additivity of Rényi entropy over coordinates. That is CLAUDE.md open issue 2 /
  PLAN.md open question 1, and §7 is about not depending on it: the joint form of the
  density bound gives `H_p(X)` directly, with no per-symbol decomposition at all.

With that caveat, `m ≈ n(1 − h) + r·h + p + log_q(1/ε)`. A field that is 10 bits larger
than necessary does not cost a
constant factor; it lowers `h`, raises `m`, and raises the worker count `λ`
super-linearly. Measured below: 4.2 wasted bits of `q` at `n = 100` cost ~5 key symbols and
~400 workers.

So the design problem is not "pick `q` big enough". It is **pick the smallest `q` that
cannot wrap, and then spend the remaining freedom on raising the entropy per field symbol.**

Three further constraints make this setting specific:

1. **Overflow is undetectable client-side.** The client uploads `x̃` and keeps only `k`. It
   never holds the data again, so at decode time a wrapped result is indistinguishable from
   a correct one — there is no invariant to check, no second party to compare against. Every
   MPC paper's "assume `q` is large enough to avoid wraparound" is, here, a *hard proof
   obligation discharged at quantisation time*. This is what forces clipping (§5).
2. **One-shot storage, many queries.** `x̃` is written once and serves every gradient step.
   Any parameter the *storage* quantisation depends on is frozen for the whole training run;
   only the polynomial changes per step. This cleanly splits the scales into storage
   parameters (`f_X`, `f_y`, clip) and per-step parameters (`f_w`).
3. **Degree budget.** The workers evaluate one polynomial of total degree `≤ d`. No
   comparisons, no bit extraction, no rounding, no branching. That rules out the entire
   class of MPC techniques built on secure truncation — which is most of them.

Related work in the group's own line quantises the **model** (weights to `±1`), not the
data: Raviv–Bitar–Yaakobi ISIT 2022, Deng–Raviv ISIT 2023, Deng–Ramkumar–Bitar–Raviv 2023.
The ICC polynomial-computation paper's footnote 2 points at the same cluster. Worth citing
as the closest prior art and distinguishing on exactly this axis: here the data is
continuous and the model is public.

The closer contrast is *within* the lineage. The 2022 paper's **signal preservation**
(Def. 3) is the alternative route to learning on private data: privatise so that
`w₁x − b₁ = w₂f(x) − b₂`, and an ordinary learning algorithm runs **unaltered** on `X̃`,
since linear/logistic regression and feedforward nets only ever see linear functionals. That
is *instance encoding*, and it is not what ICC does — ICC recovers `f(x)` exactly through a
query protocol and supports any polynomial of degree `≤ d`, at the cost of `λ` workers per
query. Note that fixed-point quantisation is **not** signal preserving (clipping and
rounding are not linear), which costs nothing here but would rule this pipeline out of the
2022 route entirely. See `docs/PREDECESSOR_PAPERS.md` §3.

One more line worth quoting in the introduction: the 2022 paper assumes discrete data
*"although the arguments can be readily adapted to the continuous case by quantization"*
(§II). That single clause is the only treatment of quantisation anywhere in the lineage.
This document is what happens when it is actually carried out — and §11's `h < 1/d` ceiling
is the reason it is not the formality that phrasing suggests.

## 2. Notation, and where each symbol comes from

Nothing in this document may silently look like paper notation when it is not, so:

**From the 2024 paper** (Deng–Ramkumar–Raviv, *Perfect Subset Privacy in Polynomial
Computation*) — the scheme structure: `λ(q,d,m) = dim RM_q(d,m)` worker count (equal to
`C(m+d,d)` only when `d < q`, its Corollary 1 — use `utils.lambda_workers`), `I_{d,m}`
information set of `RM_q(d,m)`, `G`, `k`, `x̃`.

**From the 2025 non-uniform paper** (Tarnopolsky–Deng–Ramkumar–Raviv–Cohen) — the privacy
analysis: `q, n, m, d, r`, entropy order `p`, smoothing budget `ε`, confidence `a`, leakage
bound `ε_c`, and `H_p(·)` as defined in its Appendix A with a **base-`q`** logarithm.

**Introduced by this project, not in either paper.** The whole quantisation layer is new —
neither paper embeds reals, so neither has any analogue of `B`, clipping, or scales.

| symbol | meaning | defined in |
|---|---|---|
| `h` | per-symbol entropy in `log_q` units, `H_p(X)/n` | repo convention, `utils.compute_symbol_p_entropy` |
| `n_s`, `P` | samples, features; `n = n_s(P+1)` with labels stored too | §5 |
| `f_X`, `f_y`, `f_w` | fractional bits of data, labels, weights; `f_y = f_X` required | §5 |
| `A`, `A_w` | clip bounds in σ, for data and weights | `FixedPointSpec.clip` |
| `x̂`, `ŷ`, `ŵ` | integer codes after clip-scale-round | `quantize.to_integer` |
| **`B`** | **hard bound on the magnitude of the INTEGER result, pre-reduction** | §7, `gradient_bound_*` |
| `ρ_max` | bound on the source probability density | §8, `sup_density` |
| `P_sat` | probability mass on one clip boundary | §8, `saturation_mass` |
| `L`, `q_i` | number of RNS primes and the primes themselves | §4E |

**`B` deserves the emphasis**, because the obligation it encodes has no counterpart in
either paper. Both papers start with `X` already a random variable over `F_q^n`, so nothing
can wrap and the question never arises. Their "the computation results can be reliably
decoded" is a *field-level* statement: the user recovers `g` by interpolating it from `λ`
evaluations on an information set, which needs `M` to be full rank. Quantisation adds a
**second, independent** decodability obligation at the integer level — that the true integer
result never leaves the balanced window — and `B` is the quantity that discharges it. Both
must hold; §3's R2 is the second one, and study D checks them separately.

Formally: let `ĝ_j ∈ Z` be the exact integer value of the query polynomial on the integer
codes. `B` is any number with `|ĝ_j| ≤ B` for every component `j` and every admissible
weight vector. The balanced window holds `2B+1` distinct integers, so the requirement is

```
B ≤ (q−1)/2      equivalently      q ≥ 2B + 1
```

At the recommended parameters `B = 340,787,200` and `q = 681,574,423`.

**Two notation collisions to fix before the paper.** (i) `M` is the interpolation matrix
everywhere else in this project, so the density bound is written `ρ_max` here, not `M`. (ii)
`p` is the entropy order in the paper, but `docs/PLAN.md` also uses `p` for the feature
count ("summed over `n_s·p` terms"). This document uses `P` for features, which
disambiguates only by case. Pick a third symbol before either appears in a figure caption.

## 3. Requirements

| | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **R1** | The embedding commutes with polynomial evaluation of total degree `≤ d` over `F_q` |
| **R2** | The result is exactly recoverable as a real number from one field element |
| **R3** | The proof of R2 uses only quantities the client still holds after upload (`O(1)` state) |
| **R4** | `log2 q` is as small as possible, to keep `h` high |
| **R5** | Quantisation error must not dominate the ML task |

## 4. Survey of candidate embeddings

### A. Fixed point with balanced residues — **recommended**

The map is two-stage, and it matters that they are separate:

```
reals  --clip, scale by 2^f, round-->  window of Z  --mod q-->  F_q
```

Stage one is lossy and is where the precision/range tradeoff lives. Stage two is the
canonical ring homomorphism `Z → Z/qZ`; any polynomial with integer coefficients commutes
with it, which gives R1 for free at every degree. R2 holds iff the true integer result
stays in the balanced window `[−(q−1)/2, (q−1)/2]`, which is what stage two is inverted
against. In code: `to_integer` then `codes_to_field`, inverted by `lift_balanced` then a
divide by `2^f`.

> **`q` must be prime.** Stage two needs `F_q ≅ Z/qZ` *as a ring*, and that holds only for
> prime `q`. For a proper prime power `q = p^k` (`k > 1`) it fails completely: `F_{p^k}` has
> characteristic `p`, so the image of `Z` is just the prime subfield — in `GF(2^5)` the
> integers collapse to `{0, 1}` and `3 + 5 = 6`, not 8. Fixed point cannot even get started
> there, because the element labelled `2` is not `1 + 1`, so scaling by `2^f` separates
> nothing.
>
> This is a live footgun rather than a formality: `galois` labels extension-field elements
> with integers `0 … q−1` as well, and a few operations coincide by accident
> (`GF(2)*GF(7)` prints 14), so an extension field runs to completion and returns confident
> nonsense. `quantize.require_prime_field` now rejects it, and the regression suite checks
> both directions.
>
> Note where the constraint comes from: the ICC scheme is stated over a general `F_q` and
> its Reed–Muller machinery works there. **Primality is a constraint the quantisation layer
> imposes on the scheme**, and the paper should say so. The RNS variant of §4E is the one
> way to relax it — a product of *prime* fields, `Z/∏q_i ≅ ∏F_{q_i}`, not an extension
> field.

Satisfies R1–R3. The rest of this document is about sizing it.

### B. Modular rationals with rational reconstruction

Encode `a/b` as `a · b^{-1} mod q` and recover it after decoding by continued fractions
(Wang's algorithm; see also the fault-tolerant variant of Böhm–Decker–Fieker–Steel, JSC
2016). Exact rational arithmetic, including exact division — attractive because the learning
rate and the `1/n_s` in the gradient become exact.

Rejected. Reconstruction is unique only if `|a|, |b| < sqrt(q/2)`, and denominators
*multiply* under each field operation, so a degree-2 gradient summed over `n_s` samples
pushes the denominator bound past that limit almost immediately. Worse, the failure mode is
silent in exactly the way R3 forbids: the algorithm returns *some* rational, not an error.
Fixed point makes the same magnitude constraint explicit and checkable.

### C. Logarithmic / Zech embedding

Map `x ↦ g^round(log_g x)` for a generator `g` of `F_q*`. Multiplication becomes exact and
error-free.

Rejected: addition is not defined on the image (Zech logarithms need a lookup table, not a
polynomial). A gradient is a sum. Only usable for pure-product functionals.

### D. Floating point emulated in-field

Standard in MPC (Catrina–Saxena, Aliasgari et al.) but needs comparisons and bit
decomposition for the exponent alignment, i.e. depth and branching. Violates R1 outright
under a single degree-`d` polynomial. Rejected.

### E. Residue number system / CRT over several small primes — **promoted to a first-class option**

Run `L` instances over coprime primes `q_1 … q_L` with `∏ q_i` exceeding the dynamic range,
and CRT-reconstruct at the client. Correctness is immediate — reduction mod `q_i` is a ring
homomorphism, so each instance computes the true integer result mod `q_i`.

The interesting part is entropy, and it is stronger than PLAN.md Week 8 assumed. Measured
exactly (study C), `h_i = min(H_p(symbol in bits), log2 q_i) / log2 q_i`: entropy in bits is
capped by the source, so *shrinking* the field raises the ratio. Concretely, a 4σ-clipped
Gaussian at `f_X = 12` has `H_2 = 13.83` bits, and folding it mod `q_i = 1031` gives
`h_i = 1.0000` — the residue is uniform to displayed precision.

> **Design rule.** Choose each prime with `log2 q_i ≤ H_p(symbol)` in bits. Then `h_i = 1`,
> Theorem 1 collapses to `m_i ≥ r + p + log_{q_i}(1/ε)`, and **`m` stops depending on `n`
> at all.** At `q_i = 1031, r = 5, p = 2, ε = 1e-6`: `m_i = 9`, `λ_i = 55`, `ε_c = 0.0040`.
> At `f_X = 12, f_w = 0` the dynamic range is 37.3 bits, so `L = 4` such primes suffice:
> **220 workers against 2,485** for the single-prime design at the same precision, an 11×
> reduction, with `m_i = 9` instead of `m = 69`.

Two further advantages that the plan did not anticipate:

- **It dissolves the entropy-estimation problem.** With `h_i = 1` both Theorem 1 entropy
  terms are tight and assumption-free: `H_p(X) = n` and `max_R H_p(X_R) = r`. No source
  model, no independence assumption, nothing to estimate — which is to say it also
  side-steps open question 1 (correlated features), the single largest hole in the current
  analysis. Folding a smooth joint density is near-uniformising whether or not the
  coordinates are correlated.
- **It removes the implementation ceiling of §7 entirely**, since every prime is small.

Still blocked on privacy: the provider observes all `L` instances, and the residues jointly
determine `x mod ∏ q_i`, which carries the full entropy. Per-instance bounds do not
obviously compose. That is PLAN.md open question 5, unchanged — but the payoff is now large
enough that it should be asked **before** Weeks 5–6 rather than in Week 8, because it
changes which parameters the case study should use.

### F. Non-uniform quantisers: companding, Lloyd–Max, quantile transform

A uniform quantiser is entropy-*inefficient* for a Gaussian source: it spends the same
number of levels on the tails as on the centre. A quantile (rank) transform pushes each
marginal to `Uniform[0,1]` before scaling, after which the quantised symbol is uniform over
its `2^f` levels and `H_p = f` bits **exactly**, for every order `p`, by construction.

This does **not** break R1, which is the non-obvious part. The transform is applied before
quantisation, as a *feature transform*: the model is then linear in the transformed
features, which is an ordinary and often better-behaved regression. Nothing downstream of
storage changes.

Three consequences, two good:

- Range becomes bounded by construction, so clipping and its saturation atom (§8) disappear
  and the magnitude bound tightens.
- Entropy becomes *known*, not estimated or bounded — the strongest possible position for
  Theorem 1.
- But it is a public, data-dependent transform (the empirical CDF), i.e. the same class of
  leakage as open question 4, and a stronger instance of it: the empirical CDF is a far
  richer function of the data than a per-column mean and variance.

**And there is a precedent that reframes it.** This is the continuous analogue of the
*uniformization mechanism* of the 2022 paper (§III-A), which maps a `p`-dyadic source onto
`{0,1}^d` exactly uniformly by randomised block assignment. Two things carry over. Its
randomness is **secret and client-side**, so it genuinely adds entropy — the legitimate
version of the dither idea §4G records as a trap. And its cost is quantified rather than
hand-waved: `I(X^n; X̃^n) ≥ (n−r)H(X) − r·H(X̂|X)`, with the penalty essentially unavoidable
(2022, Thm. 3).

The decisive point is *why ICC dropped it*: uniformization needs `P_X`, and ICC's premise is
that `P_X` is unknown. A quantile transform estimates `P_X` from the sample, so it
reintroduces precisely the assumption the ICC line was built to remove. **Do not adopt it
before that is resolved** — and open question 8 is better posed as the lineage question it
really is (see `docs/PREDECESSOR_PAPERS.md` §5d).

Lloyd–Max is the wrong objective here — it minimises MSE, not entropy — and the
entropy-constrained scalar quantiser literature (Gray–Neuhoff; Kreitmeier–Linder for the
Rényi-constrained version) optimises a rate-distortion tradeoff we do not have, because our
"rate" is fixed by `log2 q`.

### G. Stochastic rounding and dithering

Measured, and mostly a **negative result** worth recording so it is not re-litigated:

- **Stochastic rounding adds no entropy.** Discretised `N(0,1)` at `f = 8`: `H_2 = 9.824`
  bits under nearest rounding and `9.824` under stochastic rounding. The source is already
  smooth on the lattice scale, so the extra randomness convolves a nearly-flat local
  distribution with a triangular kernel and changes nothing. It costs `sqrt(2)` in RMS
  error. For the **stored data**, which is quantised once and never accumulates, nearest
  rounding is strictly better.
- Its real justification is unbiasedness under accumulation, which applies to the
  **weights** if low `f_w` stalls descent. Keep it available for Week 7, not for storage.
  (Convergence results: Xia–Massei et al., and Connolly–Higham on biased variants.)
- **Known dither is a trap.** Adding a uniform `u` and querying `f(z − u)` makes `x + u`
  look uniform, and it is tempting to then claim `H_p(X) = n`. It is false: `u` appears in
  the polynomial's coefficients, so the provider learns it, and conditioned on `u` the
  leakage about `X_R` is exactly what it was. **Entropy in Theorem 1 must be conditional on
  everything the provider sees.** Only secret randomness helps, and the scheme already
  spends its secret randomness optimally as `kG`.

### H. Data that is already discrete

Worth stating in the paper: integer- or categorical-valued features need no quantisation and
get `h` determined by their own alphabet. Not the case study, but it is the regime where ICC
is cheapest, and it frames quantisation as the *price* of continuous data.

## 5. The recommended design

**Storage** (`n = n_s(P+1)` symbols: the flattened feature matrix followed by the labels).

```
x̂_ij = round(clip(X_ij, ±A) · 2^f_X)        ŷ_i = round(clip(y_i, ±A) · 2^f_X)
stored as  x̂ mod q  ∈ F_q
```

Features and labels are standardised per column first, and share one `f` and one clip `A`.
Per-feature scales are possible — fold a public multiplier `2^(F − f_j − f_k)` into the
polynomial's coefficients — but they inflate the magnitude bound to the largest scale
anyway, so standardising is strictly simpler. `f_y = f_X` is required; see below.

**Per step.** The weights are quantised at their own scale and enter as *coefficients*:

```
ŵ_k = round(clip(w_k, ±A_w) · 2^f_w)

g_j(z) = Σ_i ( Σ_k z_{X(i,k)} · ŵ_k  −  2^{f_w} · z_{y(i)} ) · z_{X(i,j)}
```

Total degree 2, one polynomial per gradient component, `P` components sharing one
interpolation matrix. Two details carry weight:

- The `2^{f_w}` multiplier on the label term is a **public integer folded into the
  coefficients**. It is what brings both terms to the common scale `2^(2f_X + f_w)` without
  making the *stored* quantisation of `y` depend on `f_w`. Since storage is written once and
  `f_w` may change per step (or per Week-7 experiment), that independence is required, not
  cosmetic. It is also why `f_y = f_X`, which `gradient_bound_worst_case` asserts.
- The weights being coefficients rather than data is the structurally elegant part of the
  protocol and should be said plainly in the paper: **the workers' shares are written once
  and never touched again for the entire training run.**

**Decode.** `Client.decode_result` returns `g_j(x̂) mod q`; lift to the balanced
representative and divide by `2^(2f_X + f_w)`. The client updates `w` in float (PLAN.md
design decision 1), so scale never accumulates across iterations.

**Verified** (study D, `n = 12`, `f_X = 8`, `f_w = 4`): the decoded lifted integer equals an
exact integer oracle for every component, and the error against the float gradient evaluated
at the *dequantised* operands is `0.00e+00` — the finite-field arithmetic contributes nothing
at all. The only error is input quantisation, and at `f_w = 4` it is dominated by the weight
precision, not by `f_X`.

## 6. Storage-time ordering: `q` is sized against a budget, not a query

**The data is uploaded before any polynomial is sent.** That is not a detail of the
implementation, it is the shape of the protocol — the 2024 paper states it outright in its
computation model, *"The polynomial `f` is not known during the storage phase"* (§II-A) —
and it constrains `q` more than §7's magnitude bound suggests on its own.

At upload the client computes `x̃ = x + kG ∈ F_q^n` and the admin immediately shards it to
`λ = C(m+d, d)` workers using the information set `I_{d,m}` (`Server.store_data` →
`get_information_set(q, m, d)`). So by the time any query exists, `q`, `m` and `d` are
already baked into the stored shares. And `m` was itself derived from `q`, since Theorem 1's
terms are in `log_q` units. The storage-time chain is

```
budget  →  B  →  q  →  entropy bounds in log_q units  →  m  →  λ, G, shares
```

and every arrow points forward. Nothing downstream can revise anything upstream.

**Consequence: `q` must bound every query the client will ever send, not the one it happens
to send first.** So the thing to declare at upload is a *ceiling* — `quantize.QueryBudget`:

```python
budget = QueryBudget(n_samples=25, n_features=3, d=2,
                     x_spec=FixedPointSpec(f=8, clip=4.0),    # storage format
                     w_spec=FixedPointSpec(f=0, clip=4.0))    # CEILING on future queries
q = choose_field_size(budget.magnitude_bound()).q             # 681,574,423
```

`w_spec` here is not one gradient step's weight format. It is the largest weight format any
future step is permitted to use.

**The constraint is one-sided, which is what makes it workable.** A later query with smaller
`f_w` or smaller weights produces a smaller integer and stays safely inside the window, so
the budget only has to be an upper bound — it does not have to be tight.
`budget.assert_admissible(ŵ, f_w)` polices it, checking `f_w ≤` declared and
`max|ŵ| ≤` declared. Both conditions together re-establish the magnitude bound. The check is
cheap and uses only public quantities, so the client runs it *before sending* — which is the
only time it can, since after upload it cannot detect a violation (§3's R3).

### 6.1 Where `w_spec` comes from, given that `w` does not exist yet

`w_spec` is the model weight vector's format, `w` starts at zero, and it is built up by the
very queries `q` has to be sized for. So it cannot be *measured* at upload. It is not
measured — **it is declared.** `w_spec` is a box the client promises to stay inside, and
clipping is what keeps the promise. Nothing about the eventual weights needs to be known;
what is needed is a commitment about what will be *allowed*.

That shifts the question to whether the box is big enough, which has an a priori answer.
For standardised `X` and `y` the OLS optimum is `w* = R⁻¹ρ` with `R` the feature correlation
matrix and `ρ` the correlation vector, so `‖w*‖ ≤ ‖R⁻¹‖·‖ρ‖ ≤ 1/λ_min(R)`. Declaring
`A_w = 2` is therefore exactly the public assumption *"the design has
`λ_min(R) ≥ 1/2`"* — a statement about admissible multicollinearity, not about this
dataset. Measured on the subsample: `max|w*| = 0.60`, `cond(XᵀX) = 4.0`, peak `|w_t| = 0.60`
along the whole trajectory, every query admitted with headroom.

**Do not read `A_w` off the data.** Setting it from the observed `|w*|` is the same leak as
setting `B` from the data (§7.1), just quieter.

**`f_w` is set by convergence, not by key size** (study E), and this reverses the
recommendation an earlier draft of this document made. Optimising `f_w` on `h` alone points
at `f_w = 0`: smallest field, highest entropy ratio. It is also useless. With standardised
features the optimum is `w* = [0.19, −0.10, 0.60]`, and a unit weight step quantises two of
those three to exactly zero, so the `Xw` term of the gradient is mostly absent and descent
limit-cycles:

| `f_w` | step | quantised `w*` | excess loss vs OLS |
|---:|---:|---|---:|
| 0 | 1.0 | `[0, 0, 1]` | **+52%** |
| 2 | 0.25 | `[0.25, 0, 0.5]` | 1.8e-3 |
| 4 | 0.0625 | `[0.19, −0.13, 0.63]` | 2.8e-4 (9.0e-4 relative) |
| 6 | 0.0156 | `[0.19, −0.09, 0.61]` | 4.5e-6 |
| 8 | 0.0039 | `[0.20, −0.10, 0.60]` | 1.1e-6 |

`f_w = 0` is not a precision tradeoff, it is a dead zone — requirement R5 fails outright.
`f_w = 4` is the operating point. **The price of insisting on it is 306 extra workers**
(`λ = 3,081` against 2,775), which is the honest cost of a usable model and belongs in the
paper as such.

**Enforcement, in practice.** Clipping `w` to `A_w` at every step makes admissibility
automatic. If the true optimum lies outside `A_w`, training converges to the clipped
solution — a utility cost, not a correctness failure.

**The degree is the harsh entry, and it is structural rather than numeric.** The shares only
support evaluation of polynomials in `RM_q(d, m)`. A degree-3 query against `d = 2` shares
is not inaccurate, it is *undecodable*, and no choice of `q` repairs it. Declaring `d = 3`
up front is possible but expensive: it raises `λ = C(m+d, d)`, and by §11 it lowers the
entropy ceiling to `h < 1/3`. Committing to `d = 2` is committing to quadratic queries for
the life of the upload.

### 6.2 What repeated queries do not do
 Each query is decoded independently, so magnitudes do
not accumulate across the `T` gradient steps — every query needs `|ĝⱼ| ≤ (q−1)/2`
individually, and `T` does not enter `B`. (What *does* accumulate across steps is leakage,
which is next semester's question, not this one.)

## 7. Field size: the decodability obligation

Reduction mod `q` is a ring homomorphism, so the decoded element equals the true integer
gradient mod `q`. Lifting is exact **iff** that integer never leaves `[−(q−1)/2, (q−1)/2]`.
Hence `q ≥ 2B + 1` for a bound `B` on `|g_j|`, and by R3 that bound must be *hard*.

**Worst case, from the public format only** (`gradient_bound_worst_case`):

```
|g_j| ≤ B ≜ n_s · ( P · X̂max² · Ŵmax  +  2^{f_w} · X̂max · Ŷmax )

        X̂max = Ŷmax = ⌊A_X·2^{f_X}⌋      Ŵmax = ⌊A_w·2^{f_w}⌋
```

Since `f_y = f_X` forces `Ŷmax = X̂max`, this collapses to
`B = n_s·X̂max²·2^{f_w}·(P·A_w + 1)`, and taking logs gives the whole field size in closed
form:

```
log2 q  =  1  +  log2 n_s  +  2(f_X + log2 A_X)  +  f_w  +  log2(P·A_w + 1)
```

At the recommended parameters (`n_s = 25, P = 3, f_X = 7, A_X = 4, f_w = 4, A_w = 2`):

| term | value | what it pays for |
|---|---:|---|
| `1` | 1.00 | the factor 2 in `q ≥ 2B+1` — the sign bit |
| `log2 n_s` | 4.64 | summing over samples |
| `2(f_X + log2 A_X)` | 18.00 | **two data factors**, precision and range each |
| `f_w` | 4.00 | weight precision |
| `log2(P·A_w + 1)` | 2.81 | summing over features, and weight range |
| **total** | **30.45** | `B = 734,003,200`, `q = 1,468,006,403` |

Verified against the code: closed form 30.45, measured 30.45.

**Notice what is absent: any value from `X`.** Every term is a *shape* or *format*
parameter — how many samples, how many features, how many bits, how wide the clip. You do
not need the data to size the field, you need only the declaration of what the data will
look like. That is the whole reason this policy leaks nothing, and it is why the bound can
be computed before a single value is read.

The `2(f_X + …)` term is also where §11's ceiling comes from: two data factors in the field,
one factor's worth of entropy per stored symbol.

Clipping is what makes this exist: without it there is no a priori bound on `|X|`. Leaks
nothing, because `A`, `f_X`, `f_w`, `n_s`, `P` are all public.

Measured looseness: **680×** (9.4 bits of `q`) on the seeded synthetic case, because it
assumes every sample simultaneously attains the clip bound.

**Data-dependent** (`gradient_bound_data_dependent`): substitute the true sums over samples,
keeping the worst case only over `w`, which is not yet known. The client can evaluate this at
upload time — it still holds the data — and remember the one resulting scalar, `O(1)` state
alongside `k`. Worth 4.2 bits on the diabetes subsample, which is what lets `f_X = 10,
f_w = 0` stay under the native-arithmetic ceiling.

**But it leaks, and the arithmetic says decisively not to use it.**

### 7.1 How data leaks into `q`

`q` is public — the provider cannot do field arithmetic without it. So if `q` was computed
from the data, publishing it is a side channel, and it is one Theorem 1 does not cover:
the theorem bounds `I(X̃; X_R)` conditioned on the scheme parameters, of which `q` is one.

The channel is not vague. `q = next_prime(2B + 1)` for the smallest such prime, so knowing
`q` pins `B` to within a prime gap (average `ln q ≈ 21` at 30 bits) — effectively revealing
`B` outright, about `log2 B ≈ 29` bits. And `B` is
`max_j Σ_i (Σ_k |x̂_ik|·Ŵmax + 2^{f_w}|ŷ_i|)·|x̂_ij|`, a weighted ℓ1-type aggregate of the
entire data vector. At `n_s = 1` that is very nearly a direct read of `|x|`; at `n_s = 25` it
is an aggregate, but "aggregate" is not a guarantee, and nothing in the analysis bounds what
an `r`-subset contributes to it.

### 7.2 The rule, and why it is the boring one

`quantize.choose_field_size` implements three policies and returns the leakage accounting
alongside `q`, so the comparison cannot be skipped:

| policy | bound from | `q` leaks | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| `public-format` | clip, `f_X`, `f_w`, `n_s`, `P` only | **0 bits** | **use this** |
| `coarsened` | data, rounded up to a public fixed grid | `≤ log2|grid|` bits | not worth it |
| `data-exact` | data, published directly | `~log2 B ≈ 29` bits | refused in code |

For `coarsened` the bound is a chain-rule argument: `I(X_R; X̃, q) ≤ I(X_R; X̃) + H(q)` and
`H(q) ≤ log2|grid|`, since the only thing published is which public bucket the data fell
into. The grid must be fixed in advance — a grid chosen from the data leaks through the grid.

Now put both channels in the same units. `ε_c` is in `log_q` units, so in bits it is
`ε_c · log2 q` (`quantize.leakage_budget_bits`). Measured at `f_X = 8, f_w = 0` on the
diabetes subsample:

```
scheme's entire per-subset budget   eps_c = 0.0016  ->  0.047 bits
coarsened policy, 41-bucket grid                    ->  5.36 bits   (114x the budget)
bits of q it saves                                  ->  3.34
```

**Spending 5.4 bits of side channel to save 3.3 bits of `q` is 114× the entire leakage the
scheme exists to bound.** It is not a close call, and it settles a question the tables in §10
otherwise leave open: use the public-format bound, accept the looser `q`, and treat
`gradient_bound_data_dependent` as an instrument for *quantifying* what is given up rather
than as a configuration to ship.

Two caveats keep this from being final. `H(q)` is a worst-case bound on `I(X_R; q)`, and
since `B` is an aggregate over all `n` coordinates a sensitivity-style analysis could be far
tighter — that is open question 7, and it is the only thing that could revive the tighter
bound. And a grid coarse enough to be competitive (two or three buckets) is close enough to
the public-format policy that the distinction stops mattering.

## 8. Entropy: what to feed Theorem 1, and in which direction

Theorem 1 **subtracts** `H_p(X)` and **adds** `max_R H_p(X_R)`. So it needs a *lower* bound
on the first and an *upper* bound on the second. Either one backwards under-estimates `m`
and breaks the guarantee — and 25 samples cannot resolve a `2^18`-ary distribution, so an
empirical estimator is not available at case-study scale regardless.

**Upper bound on `max_R H_p(X_R)`, assumption-free.** Rényi entropy is maximised by the
uniform distribution, so

```
max_R H_p(X_R) ≤ r · log2(levels) = r · log2(2⌊A·2^f⌋ + 1)   bits
```

for every order and every dependence structure. No source model at all. This half of open
question 1 is simply closed.

**Lower bound on `H_p(X)` via min-entropy and a density bound.** For `p > 1`,
`Σ P_i^p ≤ (max_i P_i)^(p−1)`, so `H_p ≥ −log2 max_i P_i` — bounding the largest atom bounds
every order at once. Two kinds of atom compete:

```
interior cell:     mass ≤ 2^-f · ρ_max    →  f − log2 ρ_max      bits
saturation atom:   mass ≤ P_sat           →  −log2 P_sat         bits   (independent of f)

H_p(symbol) ≥ min( f − log2 ρ_max,  −log2 P_sat )
```

where `ρ_max` bounds the source density. For standardised features
`ρ_max = 1/sqrt(2π)`, giving
`f + 1.325` bits.

*Tightness* (study A): against the exact discretised-Gaussian entropy the interior term is
loose by **exactly 0.500 bits at `H_2`, independent of `f`** — the true value is
`f + log2(2 sqrt(π)) = f + 1.825`. Tight enough to use in place of an estimate.

*The saturation term is not optional, and it was found by falsification.* At `A = 4σ,
f = 16` the exact `H_8` is **16.93 bits, below** the interior term `17.33`: the clipped tail
atom (mass `3.2e-5`) has overtaken a typical interior cell (mass `6.1e-6`). Dropping the
saturation term there would have over-estimated `H_p(X)` and under-estimated `m` — failure
in the unsafe direction. The fix is to widen the clip, not to weaken the bound: keep
`P_sat ≤ 2^-f · ρ_max`, which needs

| `f` | 8 | 12 | 16 | 20 | 24 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| min clip (σ) | 2.96 | 3.73 | 4.37 | 4.95 | 5.46 |

Widening the clip is very cheap, because it enters the magnitude bound polynomially while
the tail mass it controls decays like a Gaussian. Measured at `n_s = 25, P = 3, f_X = 8,
f_w = 0`, relative to `A = 4σ`:

| `A` (σ) | `log2 q` | cost in bits of `q` | `−log2 P_sat` | entropy headroom gained |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4 | 29.34 | — | 14.95 | — |
| 5 | 30.29 | +0.94 | 21.73 | +6.79 |
| 6 | 31.06 | +1.72 | 29.92 | +14.97 |
| 7 | 31.72 | +2.37 | 39.51 | +24.56 |

So one extra σ buys about 7 bits of headroom for under 1 bit of `q`. `A = 4` is valid
through `f_X = 12`; past that, widen rather than weaken the bound.

**Why this matters for open question 1.** `utils.compute_max_subset_p_entropy` returns `r·h`
in closed form, exact only for independent coordinates, and Rényi entropy of order `p ≥ 2`
is not subadditive — so for correlated features `Σ h_i` bounds `H_p(X)` in neither
direction. The density bound has no such problem: applied to the **joint** density with
`ρ_max,n` bounding it,

```
H_p(X) ≥ n·f − log2 ρ_max,n   bits,      and for a Gaussian source
−log2 ρ_max,n = h_diff(X) − 0.721·n = (n/2)log2(2π) + (1/2)log2|Σ|
```

so correlation is paid for automatically through `log2|Σ|`, with no independence assumption
anywhere. It **trades an unverifiable structural assumption for a single scalar density
bound** — which is, as far as I can tell, the minimal assumption under which a quantised
continuous source's Rényi entropy can be bounded at all. `quantize.entropy_bounds` implements
both forms. It is a *proposal*; no privacy claim should rest on it before Raviv rules.

`quantize.entropy_bounds` computes **bounds**, not estimates, and is deliberately not a
third entry in the `utils.py` estimator zoo. Do not mix it with the two that are there.

## 9. Implementation ceiling: `log2 q ≤ 31.5`

`galois` keeps native `uint32` arithmetic (`ufunc_mode='jit-calculate'`) only while `q²`
fits in `int64`, i.e. `q ≤ ⌊sqrt(2^63)⌋ = 3037000499`. One prime above that it silently
switches to `dtype=object`. Measured at `λ = 1225, d = 2`:

| | build `M` | solve | `M` bytes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `q = 2^30+3` (native) | 0.15 s | 0.20 s | 6 MB |
| `q = 2^32+15` (object) | 4.27 s | 1.36 s | 11 MB |

28× and 7×. Not a wall — the build is cached per `(m, d)` — but it is the difference between
a training run in seconds and one in minutes, and it doubles the memory that PLAN.md already
identifies as the binding constraint at `n ≥ 150`.

This is a **tooling** limit, not a limit of the scheme, and the paper should say so: a
Montgomery or Barrett backend, or the RNS design of §4E, removes it. But it binds today, and
it translates into a real parameter constraint: under the worst-case magnitude bound at
`n_s = 25, P = 3, A = 4`,

```
2 f_X + f_w  ≤  ~18
```

`n = 100` (25 samples × 3 features + 25 labels), `r = 5`, `p = 2`, `ε = 1e-6`, `d = 2`,
`A = 4σ`, diabetes subsample. Full grid in `results/quantization_budget.csv`.

|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|:--:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 6 | 0 | 25.3 | 0.289 | 76 | 3,003 | yes | 21.2 | 0.346 | 71 | 2,628 |
| 8 | 0 | 29.3 | 0.318 | 73 | 2,775 | yes | 25.2 | 0.370 | 68 | 2,415 |
| 8 | 4 | 33.3 | 0.280 | 77 | 3,081 | no | 29.2 | 0.319 | 73 | 2,775 |
| 10 | 0 | 33.3 | 0.340 | 71 | 2,628 | no | 29.2 | 0.388 | 67 | 2,346 |
| 12 | 0 | 37.3 | 0.357 | 69 | 2,485 | no | 33.2 | 0.401 | 65 | 2,211 |
| 12 | 8 | 45.3 | 0.294 | 75 | 2,926 | no | 41.2 | 0.324 | 72 | 2,701 |

**Precision is better than free, and that is a refinement of the plan, not a contradiction
of it.** PLAN.md derives `sign(dh/df) = sign(k − 3c)` with `k = log2(2 n_s P)` and `c` the
per-symbol entropy overhead, and concluded "flat" from `k = 6.49` vs `3c = 5.5`. The
constant was too small: the magnitude bound also carries the clip range, so
`k = log2(2 n_s (P A² A_w + A²)) = 13.3`, while the rigorous entropy bound gives `c = 1.325`
and `3c = 4.0`. So `k − 3c > 0` comfortably and `h` **rises** with `f_X`
(`0.289 → 0.357` over `f_X = 6 → 12`). The formula in the plan is right; the parameters it
was evaluated at were not. Raise `f_X` as far as the `2f_X + f_w ≤ 18` ceiling allows.

**`f_w` is the expensive knob**, as PLAN.md Week 7 predicted: it enters `log2 q` once
without contributing anything to the numerator of `h`. Going `f_w = 8 → 0` at `f_X = 8` is
worth `h: 0.250 → 0.318` and `λ: 3,321 → 2,775`.

**Recommended case-study parameters**, revised after study E:

```
f_X = f_y = 7      A_X = 4σ       f_w = 4       A_w = 2       d = 2
q = 1,468,006,403  (log2 q = 30.45, native)     B = 734,003,200
h = 0.273          m = 77         λ = 3,081     ε_c = 0.0016
```

The table above, read on `h` alone, points at `f_X = 8, f_w = 0` (`h = 0.318`, `λ = 2,775`).
**That configuration does not train** — study E measures it 52% above the OLS loss, because
a unit weight step annihilates the optimum. `f_w = 4` is the smallest precision that works,
and once `f_w = 4` is fixed the native ceiling `log2 q ≤ 31.5` caps `f_X` at 7. `A_w = 2`
rather than 4 because it costs nothing here — same `m`, same `λ` — while doubling the
headroom over the measured peak `|w_t| = 0.60`.

So the operating point is not chosen by maximising `h`; it is the `h`-best point that
*also* satisfies R5 and stays under the tooling ceiling. Worth presenting that way, because
it is the moment the privacy metric and the utility metric actually conflict.

## 11. The structural ceiling: `h < 1/d`

This is the most important limitation of the whole approach, and it is not a tuning problem.

A degree-`d` computation over data quantised at `2^f` produces results of magnitude
`~N·(A·2^f)^d`, so

```
log2 q  ≈  d·f + d·log2 A + log2(2N)
```

while one stored symbol carries only `H_p ≈ f + c` bits. Therefore

```
h  =  H_p / log2 q  ≈  (f + c) / (d·f + const)   ->   1/d      as f grows
```

Measured, clip `6σ` so the interior entropy term always binds:

| `f_X` | 8 | 12 | 16 | 24 | 32 | 48 | 64 | limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `h`, `d = 2` | 0.348 | 0.383 | 0.405 | 0.431 | 0.445 | 0.462 | 0.471 | **0.5** |
| `h`, `d = 3` | 0.249 | 0.282 | — | 0.305 | — | 0.318 | — | **0.333** |

Monotone increasing in `f`, and asymptotically `1/d` from below. The reason is simple: the
field has to hold a product of `d` quantised factors, but each *stored symbol* is only one
of those factors, so the field is intrinsically `d` times larger than the entropy it
carries.

**Consequence.** With a single prime field, `m ≥ n(1 − 1/d) + …`, so at `d = 2` the key is
always **more than half the data length**. The `m ≪ n` regime of the paper's Example 1
(which assumes `H_p(X) = n − 1`, i.e. data nearly uniform over `F_q`) is *unreachable* by
fixed point over one prime, at any precision, for any dataset. Every row of §10 is consistent
with this: the best `h` in the whole grid is 0.401.

This should be stated plainly in the paper rather than buried, because it is the honest
answer to "does ICC work on real-valued data" — it works, correctly and verifiably, but the
storage overhead is `Θ(n)` rather than `o(n)`, and quantisation is the reason.

It is also the argument that turns §4E from an optimisation into a necessity. RNS breaks the
ceiling because it decouples the two roles of `q`: the *product* `∏ q_i` covers the dynamic
range while each *individual* `q_i` is chosen no larger than the per-symbol entropy, giving
`h_i = 1` and `m_i` independent of both `n` and `d`. Nothing else in the design space does
that.

## 12. Open questions

Continuing the numbering in `docs/PLAN.md` (1–6 there). Questions 1 and 5 there are both
directly affected by this document — 1 has a proposed resolution in §8, and 5 is now more
valuable than its Week-8 slot suggests.

7. **Does choosing `q` from the data leak?** The data-dependent magnitude bound is worth 4.2
   bits of `q` (≈5 key symbols, ≈400 workers at `n = 100`), but it makes the public field
   size a function of the private data, which Theorem 1 does not model. Is there an accepted
   way to account for it — coarsening `q` to a public grid and charging the leaked bits
   somewhere, or conditioning the theorem on `q`?
8. **Is a quantile/rank pre-transform acceptable?** It makes the per-symbol entropy exactly
   `f` bits by construction, removes clipping and its saturation atom, and needs no source
   model — the strongest possible input to Theorem 1. But the empirical CDF is a much richer
   data-dependent public transform than the standardisation of open question 4.
9. **Is the min-entropy/density lower bound (§6) the intended way to instantiate `H_p(X)`
   for continuous data?** It is rigorous, order-uniform, survives correlation, and is
   measured loose by 0.5 bits for a Gaussian. If it is acceptable, open question 1's
   independence assumption can be dropped rather than patched.

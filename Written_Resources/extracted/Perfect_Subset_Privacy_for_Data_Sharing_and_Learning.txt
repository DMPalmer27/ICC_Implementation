Abstract—As the size of modern datasets grows, it becomes
increasingly common to delegate computational tasks to ser-
vice providers. Doing so, however, raises privacy concerns.
Privatization schemes which enable learning algorithms to be
executed unaltered have been recently popularized under the
name instance encoding, aiming to circumvent the large overhead
of traditional cryptographic primitives. In this work we take an
information/coding-theoretic approach towards instance encod-
ing. Speciﬁcally, recent works have shown that general-purpose
data sharing can be achieved without leaking information about
any individual datapoint (marginally), while maintaining high
mutual information with the dataset in its entirety. We ﬁrst extend
this framework to capture the entire privacy-utility tradeoff,
accounting for the privatization of any subset of the dataset,
and provide a coding scheme for doing so. Second, we introduce
a necessary algebraic condition for applying unaltered learning
algorithms on encrypted data, termed signal preservation , and
present an additional scheme which guarantees it. Both schemes
achieve almost maximal mutual information with the entire
dataset, under appropriate assumptions. The construction relies
on some classic ideas such as Shamir secret sharing, as well as
a novel technique called random Hadamard coding.
I. I NTRODUCTION
Collecting and processing large amounts of user data is
central to many modern technologies. As data becomes more
complex, there is a growing need for sharing it with a service
provider that can cost-effectively store and process it. How-
ever, datasets often contain sensitive information, and as such,
they must be privatized before being shared. In cases where
the data is shared for learning, it is further desired to preserve
an appropriate notion of learnability post privatization. The
latter has been addressed in a series of recent works under
the term instance encoding [1, 2, 4, 5, 9, 14] which refers
to encrypting the dataset (into a privatized version thereof
of the same size) prior to sharing it. By doing so, common
learning algorithm can be executed as-is, as opposed to the
large overhead induced by techniques such as homomorphic
encryption [3, 8, 10] (see Section I-B for details).
In this paper, we address both the general-purpose and
learning-oriented dataset privatization questions by proposing
two schemes, one for each situation. Both schemes guarantee
information-theoretic subset privacy, deﬁned shortly, while
maintaining almost maximal informativeness of the privatized
dataset (up to a penalty due to data uniformization). The latter
scheme also preserves an algebraic notion of learnability we
call signal preservation.
A privatization scheme is a (possibly randomized) transfor-
mation of the original dataset X n := (X1,...,X n) into its
privatized version ~X n. A scheme is called perfectly private
if the transformed and original datasets have zero mutual
information, i.e., I(X n; ~X n) = 0 . Clearly, perfect privacy is
of no use for data sharing, since the data processing inequality
(DPI) implies that privatized data cannot be used for inference
on the original one. Yet, [11] recently showed that perfect
privacy can guaranteed for each individual datapoint, i.e.,
I(Xi; ~X n) = 0 , while maintaining high mutual information
with the original dataset as a whole. This reveals a potential
information-theoretic privacy-utility tradeoff. Can this instance
be generalized to perfect privacy of any subset of datapoints
up to a given size while maintaining high informativness?
A. Contributions
We provide herein an afﬁrmative answer to the question
above, as delineated next. First, we propose a general-purpose
data privatization mechanism that guarantees zero mutual
information between the privatized data and any subset of up
to r original datapoints. The mechanism ﬁrst uniformizes the
data by mapping it into a large enough ﬁnite ﬁeld. The trans-
formed datapoints are then linearly combined with random
keys as in Reed-Solomon encoding so as to guarantee privacy,
similar to Shamir secret sharing [12]. While this guarantees
r-subset privacy, we simultaneously aim to keep the mutual
information with the entire dataset, i.e., I(X n; ~X n), as high as
possible. We prove that the proposed schemes maximize this
mutual information, subject to the subset privacy constraint
and the uniformization mechanism. An attempt at furnishing
such a scheme was made in [11, Corollary 4.1], but privacy
guarantees were provided only to subsets of consecutive dat-
apoints—a somewhat unnatural restriction. Our mechanism,
which differs from that of [11], closes this gap, and enables
privatization of arbitrary r-subsets.
We then move to the instance encoding setup, where the
goal is to privatize the dataset such that it is not only
informative but is also well adapted for learning. Motivated by
the popularity of deep neural nets for statistical inference, we
propose signal preservation as the desired notion of learnabil-
ity. Here ‘signal’ refers to linear transformations of feature
vectors, while ‘preservation’ means that any signal that can
be obtained from the original data is also obtainable from the
privatized version (with possibly different parameters). Signal

Our second main contribution is an adaptation of the

The adaptation employs random Hadamard encoding in ad-

instance-encoding mechanism that is r-subset private, nearly
maximally informative, and preserves signals for learning.
B. Related Work and Instance Encoding
Privacy preserving computation on encrypted data is a well-
studied topic. When the data is distributed, techniques such as
multiparty computing [6] and Lagrange coded computing [15]
provide schemes with rigorous privacy guarantees that are
often amenable to collusion of parties up to a certain threshold.
Homomorphic encryption (HE) is a popular alternative for the
two-party case, and several successful attempts of incorporat-
ing it into a learning framework have been reported [3, 8, 10].
Yet, these approaches are mired by the intricate nature of
HE, as well as the drastic computation and communication
overheads. In addition, HE provides computational rather than
information-theoretic privacy.
Instance Encoding has been recently posed as an approach
to circumvent the overhead of HE and multiparty computing
while maintaining privacy (e.g., experimentally against known
attacks), even though the terminology is yet somewhat unset-
tled. The award-winning work InstaHide [9] offered the ﬁrst
such discernible attempt. By linearly combining the private
datapoints with public ones, along with random bit-ﬂipping,
[9] makes certain privacy claims and demonstrates marginal
accuracy loss (although the former has been repeatedly chal-
lenged since [1, 4, 5]). Similar privacy claims have been made
by NeuraCrypt [14], and then challenged by [2]. Additional
data transformation mechanisms have been studied in [16, 17],
analyzed via the PAC learning framework, and incorporated
differential-privacy guarantees in the single data-owner case.
Prior works in this genre vary in their assumptions and
problem setup. Variations include the existence of a public
dataset that can be utilized for encoding [9], an encoding
function that is available to the attacker [1], or an attacker
which may freely choose the data distribution [1]. The authors
are not aware of any prior attempts to formulate an instance
encoding framework in an information-theoretic or algebraic
way (see Remark 1) and the paper at hand does so based on
the notions of perfect subset privacy and signal preservation.
We emphasize that our work addresses a two-party setting
(data owner and a service provider), does not incorporate
public datapoints, and relies on a choice of a random key
known only to the data owner. We use the term “instance
encoding” to describe a signal-preserving privatization scheme
whose output is a dataset of the same size as the original. As
described in the next section. our goal is to guarantee perfect
subset privacy and maximizing mutual information with the
original dataset.

IV. L EARNING -ORIENTED PRIVATIZATION MECHANISM
Maintaining high mutual information between the original
and private datasets is a holistic approach. It does not account
for how that information could be extracted or using which
processing algorithms. The instance encoding framework aims
to address this issue and enable certain classes of algorithms
to access the information through the privacy mechanism.
We next formulate a necessary condition for a learning
algorithm to access that information. The goal is to capture
the idea that learning tasks that were possible on the original
dataset are still possible after it is privatized.
Deﬁnition 2 (Hypothesis class preservation). Let f :X1→
X2 be a mapping between feature spaces and consider two
hypothesis classes H1 andH2 of functions from X1 orX2,
respectively, to a label space Y. The map f is said to be
(H1,H2)-preserving if for any h1∈H 1 there exists h2∈H 2
such that h1(x) =h2◦f(x), for every x∈X 1.
Deﬁnition 2 concerns preserving learnability when mapping
between two ﬁxed hypothesis classes. However, different
learning algorithms use different hypothesis classes (e.g.,
depending on architecture), and optimally, we would like to
account for learnability of multiple models. To that end, we
note that a variety of models can be represented as h(x) =
¯h(Wx⊺−v⊺), i.e., they ﬁrst apply a linear transformation on
the input and only then proceed with further processing. This
includes, among others, linear classiﬁcation, logistic regres-
sion, and feedforward neural networks. Restricting attention
to such models, the next deﬁnition considers mappings that
preserve the signals wx⊺−b, for an arbitrary weight vector
w and scalar b, so as to enable learning the entire model.
Deﬁnition 3 (Signal preservation). A map f : Rd1 → Rd2
is signal preserving if for every (w1,b 1) ∈ Rd1+1 there
exists (w2,b 2)∈ Rd2+1, such that
w1x⊺−b1 =w2f(x)⊺−b2, ∀x∈ Rd1.
If the privatization mechanism is signal-preserving, then
any learning algorithm that is based on ﬁnding signals (say,
to minimize a loss function), can be executed as-is on the
privatized data. The optimization problem would now seek
the new signals rather than the original ones (e.g., (w2,b 2)
instead of (w1,b 1) in Def. 3). A potential disadvantage of this
approach is increased complexity and risk of overﬁtting, which
are beyond the scope of this paper. One can readily verify that
any invertible linear transform is signal preserving, and that
signal-preserving maps are closed under composition.
We next devise a signal-preserving uniformization and en-
cryption schemes. This results in an end-to-end signal preserv-
ing privatization mechanism due to the composition property.

An r-point privacy mechanism should keep I(X n; ~X n) as

necessary? To answer this question, we set up an information-
theoretic framework that captures a broad class of r-point
privacy mechanisms and derive an upper bound onI(X n; ~X n)
under this framework. The set up is as follows.
1) Dataset: Recall that X n contains i.i.d. copies of X∼ PX.
2) Random keys: We assume the availability of individual and
shared random keys. Let B1,...,B n be individual keys (i.e.,
each Bi corresponds to Xi) that are conditionally i.i.d. given
the datapoints according to PB|X. Further let K be a shared
key (i.e., the same K is used for all Xi’s) independent of
(X n,B n) with distribution PK.
3) Encryption functions: For every i∈ [n], let fi :X×B→
